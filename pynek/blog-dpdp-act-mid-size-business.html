DPDP Act, 2023: what actually changes for a mid-size Indian business

The obligations are not the hard part. Finding every place personal data sits, and being able to delete it, usually is.

5 August 2026 8 min read

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies to organisations processing the digital personal data of people in India, and to processing outside India where it relates to offering goods or services within India. For a mid-size business, most of the Act reads as common sense: tell people what you collect, collect it for a stated purpose, keep it secure, delete it when the purpose ends, and respond when they ask.

The difficulty is almost never the text of the law. It is the distance between what an organisation believes it does with personal data and what its systems actually do.

The inventory is the whole job

Every obligation in the Act follows the data. You cannot limit a purpose you have not recorded, honour an erasure request for data you cannot locate, or notify a breach affecting a system you did not know held personal data.

In practice the inventory is always larger than expected. The CRM and the HR system are known. What emerges during discovery is the rest: a support desk holding identity documents in ticket attachments, three spreadsheets on a shared drive, an analytics platform storing user identifiers, a marketing tool a team signed up for with a company card, backup archives nobody has opened since 2021, and a processor in another country holding a full copy of the customer table.

If you do only one thing this quarter, build the inventory. Every other obligation becomes tractable once it exists, and none of them are tractable before.

Consent has to be as easy to withdraw as it was to give

Consent under the Act is expected to be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given by a clear affirmative action, and accompanied by a notice that itemises what is being collected and for what purpose.

Two patterns commonly fail. The first is bundling — a single checkbox covering service delivery, marketing and sharing with partners, where declining is not possible without declining the service. The second is asymmetric withdrawal: consent given with one click at signup, withdrawal requiring an email to an address that is not monitored. Withdrawal must be as easy as giving it was.

Rights requests need a process, not goodwill

Data principals can seek access to a summary of their data and the processing, correction and completion, erasure, and grievance redressal, and they may nominate another individual to exercise these rights.

The technical test is simple: pick a real customer record and try to answer, end to end, where every copy of it lives. Most organisations discover that access is achievable, correction is achievable in the primary system but not in the copies, and erasure is not achievable at all without manual work across several platforms. Build and test that process before a request arrives with a clock attached.

Erasure is the obligation people underestimate

Deleting a row in the production database is the easy part. The same record is usually also present in nightly backups, in the data warehouse, in log aggregation, in the CRM’s audit trail, in the support ticketing system, in exported reports on a shared drive, and with any processor holding a copy.

Backups deserve a documented position rather than an improvised one: define the retention cycle, record that deleted records are suppressed on restore, and be able to explain the approach. A defensible, documented process is a far better position than a policy claiming immediate deletion everywhere that your architecture does not support.

Security safeguards are an obligation, not best practice

The Act requires reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach, and a breach obliges notification to the Data Protection Board and to affected data principals.

The notification duty has a practical consequence that is easy to miss: you can only report what you can detect. An organisation without adequate logging does not have fewer breaches, it has undetected ones — and an inability to establish what happened is not a defence. Detection capability is part of meeting the obligation, not separate from it.

Your processors are your exposure

Where you engage processors, the obligations to data principals remain yours. That makes processor contracts and their actual security posture part of your compliance position, not theirs alone.

Review what each processor holds, what the contract says about purpose limitation, sub-processing, breach notification timing and deletion on termination, and whether they can actually execute an erasure request you pass on. Vendor contracts signed before the Act often say nothing useful on any of these points.

A workable sequence

  • Build the inventory and processing map first.
  • Fix notices and consent mechanics for the flows carrying the most data.
  • Build and test the rights request process against a real record.
  • Define retention periods per purpose and work out what erasure requires in each system.
  • Close the detection gaps that would prevent you identifying a breach.
  • Review processor contracts and their practical ability to comply.
