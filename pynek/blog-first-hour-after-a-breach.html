What to do in the first hour after you suspect a breach

The decisions made before an examiner is involved usually determine what an examination can establish afterwards.

19 August 2026 6 min read

Most forensic examinations that fail to answer the client’s question do not fail for technical reasons. They fail because of what happened in the hours between somebody noticing a problem and somebody calling an examiner. This is the checklist we give over the phone, in the order it matters.

Do not power-cycle or reinstall the machine

The instinct is to reboot it, clean it, or reimage it and move on. Each of those destroys evidence, and the first is worse than people expect.

A running machine holds volatile memory containing the processes that are actually executing, network connections that are currently open, injected code that exists nowhere on disk, and in many cases encryption keys and credentials in cleartext. A reboot discards all of it. A reinstall discards the rest.

Isolate the machine from the network rather than shutting it down. Pull the cable, disable the wireless adapter, or move the switch port to a quarantine VLAN. You keep the volatile evidence and you stop the spread.

Preserve logs before retention windows expire

This is the step with a deadline attached, and it is the one most often missed. Firewall logs are commonly retained for thirty to ninety days. Cloud sign-in logs are frequently kept for less. Dwell time in a real intrusion routinely exceeds both, which means the evidence of initial access can expire while the investigation is still being authorised.

On the day you suspect something: extend retention on every source you control, and export what already exists to somewhere outside the affected environment. Firewall, proxy, DNS, VPN, authentication, mail platform audit logs and endpoint telemetry. Export first, decide what is relevant later.

If CCTV is relevant, stop the recorder from overwriting. A four-day retention cycle is not unusual, and a DVR does not pause while a decision is made.

Start a contemporaneous record

From the moment you suspect something, write down what is happening: who noticed what and at what time, who touched which system, what they did, and what they observed. Note the times from a reliable clock, not from memory afterwards.

This record is worth considerably more than people expect. It establishes when you became aware — which matters for regulatory notification — and it lets an examiner distinguish your team’s activity from the intruder’s. Without it, every administrative action during the response period becomes an ambiguity in the timeline.

Do not tip off an insider

Where the concern is an employee rather than an external attacker, the response order changes. Confronting the person, disabling their account, or asking IT to “have a look at their laptop” all give notice, and notice is usually followed by deletion.

The sequence that works is to preserve first and confront second: secure the device and the account’s mail and file history quietly, then act. Take advice on employment and privacy obligations before examining a personal device or a personal account — authority to examine is a separate question from technical ability to do so, and it is one your counsel should answer.

Be careful with the “quick look”

Someone will offer to check the machine first. Browsing the file system changes access timestamps. Running an antivirus scan can quarantine or delete the very artefact that explains the incident. Copying files out with ordinary tools produces a copy without any of the metadata that makes it evidential.

If a preliminary look is genuinely necessary, document who did it, when, and exactly what they ran. An examiner can work around known contamination. Unknown contamination is what causes a finding to be challenged.
