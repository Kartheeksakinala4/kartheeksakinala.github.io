DPDP Compliance

Obligations follow the data, so the data comes first

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies to you if you process the digital personal data of people in India — and to processing outside India where it relates to offering goods or services within India. Most of what the Act asks for is unremarkable in principle: tell people what you collect, collect it for a stated purpose, keep it secure, delete it when the purpose ends, and respond when they ask.

The difficulty is almost never the law. It is that nobody has a complete picture of where personal data actually sits — the CRM, the support desk, three spreadsheets, the analytics platform, the backup archive nobody has opened since 2021, and the processor in another country your marketing team signed up for last quarter.

We start with a data inventory and processing map, assess it against the Act's obligations, and hand you a remediation plan ordered by exposure rather than by section number. Where you act as a Data Fiduciary through processors, the contractual gaps usually matter more than the technical ones.

Coverage

What the assessment covers

Data inventory and flow mapping

Every system, spreadsheet, SaaS tool and backup holding personal data — what is collected, why, where it goes, who can see it and how long it stays.

Notice and consent

Whether your notices are specific, itemised and available in the languages the Act contemplates, and whether consent is free, informed, unconditional and as easy to withdraw as it was to give.

Data principal rights

Your ability to actually answer an access, correction, erasure or grievance request within a defensible timeframe — including finding every copy of the record.

Retention and erasure

Defined retention periods tied to purpose, and a deletion process that reaches backups, logs, analytics platforms and processors rather than just the primary database.

Security safeguards

The technical and organisational measures the Act requires you to maintain — access control, encryption, logging, and the controls that would actually detect a breach.

Breach response

A tested process for identifying a personal data breach and notifying the Data Protection Board and affected data principals, with the evidence trail to show what happened when.

Questions

What clients ask first

Is this legal advice?

No. This is a technical and process assessment of how your systems handle personal data, and it is prepared to be reviewed alongside advice from your counsel. We will tell you where a question is legal rather than technical — classification as a Significant Data Fiduciary, for instance — and flag it for your lawyers rather than answering it ourselves.

We already did GDPR work. Does that carry over?

A great deal of it does — inventory, mapping, retention discipline and security safeguards transfer almost directly. The differences that matter are in consent mechanics, the notice requirements, the rights framework and grievance handling, and breach notification to the Data Protection Board of India. We assess against the DPDP Act specifically rather than assuming equivalence.

What usually turns out to be the biggest gap?

Erasure. Organisations can generally find personal data when asked, but very few can delete it everywhere — backups, log aggregation, analytics warehouses, CRM audit trails and the processors holding copies. That is normally the longest item on the remediation plan.
