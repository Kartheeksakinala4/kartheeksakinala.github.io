Network Forensics

The host says what happened to it; the network says where else it went

A compromised endpoint tells you about itself. The network tells you the shape of the whole incident — which host was first, which credentials were used to move sideways, which internal systems were touched, which external destinations received traffic, and how long it went on before anyone noticed.

We work from whatever record exists: firewall and proxy logs, NetFlow, DNS query logs, VPN and authentication records, endpoint telemetry, and full packet capture where it was being retained. In most environments the answer is assembled from partial sources, and the report says which conclusions rest on which evidence.

Where the question is data loss, the assessment is deliberately careful. Volume moved to an external destination is evidence of transfer; it is not by itself evidence of what was in it. Where the content cannot be established, we say so rather than allowing an inference that will not hold when someone tests it.

Coverage

What we reconstruct

Initial access

The entry point: an exposed service, a VPN login from an unexpected source, a phishing click, or a supplier connection nobody was monitoring.

Lateral movement

Which accounts were used to reach which hosts, in what order, over which protocols — the path from the first machine to the systems that actually mattered.

Command and control

Beacon detection in flow and DNS data, including low-and-slow channels and traffic tunnelled inside protocols your perimeter treats as ordinary.

Data movement

What volume went where and when, staging locations inside the network, archive creation, and the destinations that received traffic.

Authentication anomalies

Impossible travel, credential reuse across hosts, service accounts logging on interactively, and authentication patterns inconsistent with normal operation.

Incident timeline

A single time-ordered account of the intrusion from first access to containment, correlated across every available source.

Questions

What clients ask first

We do not keep packet captures. Is an investigation still possible?

Yes. Very few organisations retain full capture, and most network investigations are built from firewall, proxy, DNS, VPN, authentication and endpoint logs. Those establish who connected to what, when, from where and how much moved. Capture adds content; its absence limits certain conclusions about what was in a transfer, and the report will state that limitation rather than work around it.

How far back can you go?

As far as your retention allows, which is the binding constraint in almost every case. Firewall logs are often kept 30 to 90 days, cloud sign-in logs frequently less, and dwell time in a real intrusion routinely exceeds both. The single most useful thing you can do on the day you suspect something is extend retention and export everything before it rolls off.

Can you tell us exactly what data was taken?

Only where the evidence supports it. Flow records establish volume, destination and timing. Content requires packet capture, proxy body logging or corroborating host-side evidence such as the archive that was staged before transfer. Where those exist we say what was taken; where they do not, we report transfer without content attribution rather than infer it.
